03 · when no matrix works

The functions that ruin the matrix representation.

Some operations can't be folded into any K, no matter how you choose it. Lens distortion, fisheye projection, rolling shutter, panoramas. The single litmus test that tells you in advance: does the operation keep straight lines straight?

One test decides everything

You don't have to memorise a list of what breaks K and what doesn't. There's a single question that settles it:

The litmus test

Does the operation keep straight lines straight? If yes, it's a collineation — a projective-linear map — and it can be folded into the matrix (K′ = A·K). If it bends a straight line, no 3×3 matrix can represent it, full stop. K is built from matrix multiplies, and matrix multiplies can't curve a line. Everything below fails the test.

The reason traces straight back to page one. The linear model is central projection (every ray through one pinhole) followed by an affine read-out of a flat plane. Both halves preserve straightness. Break either — curve the rays, or move the pinhole — and you've left the world a single K can describe.

Lens distortion — the rays curve

A real lens is not a perfect pinhole. Light through curved glass bends a little extra, more so toward the edges, so a straight line in the world bows on the sensor. Two flavours: barrel (edges bulge out) and pincushion (edges pinch in).

It's modelled, but not with K. Distortion is a polynomial nudge applied to the normalized point before K converts to pixels. The standard Brown–Conrady model adds a radial term (depends on distance r from centre) and a tangential term (from lens-sensor misalignment):

xd = x(1 + k1r² + k2r⁴ + k3r⁶) + [2p1xy + p2(r²+2x²)]

with r² = x²+y². Those r², r⁴, r⁶ powers are the whole point: they're polynomial in the coordinates, and no linear map has powers in it. That's why distortion lives in its own (k1, k2, k3, p1, p2) coefficients alongside K, never inside it.

How practice copes

The standard move is undistort, then use K. You apply the inverse distortion to warp the image back to what an ideal pinhole would have seen — straight lines straight again — and only then is the clean linear K valid. Calibration (e.g. OpenCV's calibrateCamera) estimates K and the distortion coefficients together, precisely because you can't separate "where the centre is" from "how the edges bow" without solving for both at once.

Fisheye — the projection law itself is different

Distortion is a perturbation of the pinhole. Fisheye is a different animal: a different projection law on purpose. The pinhole maps an incoming ray at angle θ from the axis to radius r = f·tan(θ) on the sensor. Fisheye lenses deliberately use something tamer, most commonly the equidistant law r = f·θ.

Pinhole: r = f·tan θ

As θ → 90°, tan(θ) → ∞. A 180° field of view would need an infinitely large sensor. The pinhole model literally cannot see a full hemisphere — it's not a limitation of the matrix's parameters, it's the geometry.

Fisheye: r = f·θ

Finite at 90° and beyond — a 180°+ field of view fits on a real sensor. The price: straight lines bow dramatically. Other laws exist too (equisolid, stereographic), each its own curve.

Because the projection law is non-linear in θ, there is no K — and no distortion polynomial bolted onto a pinhole — that captures a true fisheye well. This is why toolkits ship a separate fisheye model (OpenCV's cv::fisheye namespace) with its own calibration and its own four coefficients, rather than reusing the pinhole path.

Rolling shutter — there isn't one camera, there are hundreds

Most CMOS sensors don't expose every pixel at once; they read out row by row, top to bottom, each row a fraction of a millisecond after the last. If the camera or subject moves during that sweep, each row was taken by a camera in a slightly different pose. A single static K[R | t] describes one camera at one instant — but a rolling-shutter frame is a stack of hundreds of them, one per row.

The visible symptoms are the giveaway: vertical poles lean (the "skew" effect), fast wheels turn into ellipses, propellers melt into wobbling blobs. None of that is a property a 3×3 matrix can hold, because the matrix has no axis for time. The fix is a model that makes the pose a function of row, not a single matrix.

The one that looks like it should break K but doesn't

Geometric vs photometric

Vignetting — the darkening toward image corners — feels like a lens defect that ought to mess with K. It doesn't. Vignetting changes pixel brightness, not pixel position. A 3D point still lands at exactly the pixel K predicts; it's just dimmer there. That's a photometric effect, orthogonal to the geometric model K describes. The test still works: vignetting doesn't bend any line, so it leaves K untouched. Keep the two axes separate — geometry is "where," photometry is "how bright."

The map of what breaks it

Sorted by the litmus test:

  • Foldable into K′ = A·K — crop, resize, flip, in-plane rotation, shear. Lines stay straight.
  • Geometric, breaks K (needs extra non-linear model) — radial/tangential distortion, fisheye and other wide projections, rolling shutter, panorama/equirectangular stitching (multiple viewpoints, so not even central), and any per-pixel warp field. Lines bend, or the single-pinhole assumption fails.
  • Doesn't touch K at all — vignetting, white balance, blur, noise, exposure. Photometric, not geometric.
Why "lines stay straight" is the exact right test, not a rough heuristic

A map of projective space that sends collinear points to collinear points is called a collineation, and the fundamental theorem of projective geometry says (over the reals, in dimension ≥ 2) every collineation is a projective-linear transformation — i.e. multiplication by an invertible matrix in homogeneous coordinates. So "preserves straight lines" and "is representable by a matrix" are not merely correlated; they're the same condition. That's why the test is exact: an operation can be absorbed into K (as some A·K) if and only if it is a collineation of the image plane. Distortion fails it pointwise — a straight world line images to a curve — and that single fact is a complete proof that no K exists for it.

source Hartley & Zisserman ch. 2–3 (projective transformations); OpenCV docs (distortion, fisheye).

Where this leaves you

The linear model is a deal: give up curved rays, moving pinholes, and time, and in return you get a single clean matrix that you can crop, scale, flip and rotate just by multiplying. Most of computer vision takes that deal — and handles the exceptions by undistorting first, so that by the time K is applied, the world really does look like it came through a pinhole. K isn't the whole camera. It's the honest, linear core that everything else is measured against.
