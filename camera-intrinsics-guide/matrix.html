01 · the matrix itself

Every entry of K, and a knob for each.

K is a 3×3 upper-triangular matrix with five meaningful numbers. Here's what each one physically means — and a live demo where you drag fx, fy, cx, cy, and skew and watch a 3D scene re-project in real time.

The matrix, and what's actually in it

Here it is. Five numbers that matter, four structural zeros, and a 1 in the corner:

fxscx 0fycy 001
K — the intrinsic matrix

It turns the flat normalized-plane point (x, y, 1) into a pixel by a single multiply:

u = fx·x + s·y + cx     v = fy·y + cy

Read that as two lines of plain algebra. The v equation is a scale-and-shift of y. The u equation is a scale-and-shift of x, plus a little leak from y through the skew term. Every entry has a job:

  • fx, fy — focal lengths, in pixels. How many pixels one unit on the normalized plane spans. Big focal length = narrow field of view = "zoomed in." They differ only when pixels aren't square.
  • cx, cy — the principal point, in pixels. Where the optical axis punches through the sensor. Nominally the image centre, but never exactly, because the sensor is never glued on perfectly.
  • s — skew. A shear term that's non-zero only if the sensor's rows and columns aren't perpendicular. For any camera you'll meet, s = 0. It earns its slot in textbooks and almost nowhere else.
Why it's triangular

That tidy upper-triangular shape isn't a coincidence — it's what lets you separate the camera's lens from its pose. Any well-behaved projection matrix factors uniquely (via RQ decomposition) into an upper-triangular K times an orthogonal rotation. Triangular captures "the lens"; orthogonal captures "where it's pointing." Full detail in the deep-dive below.

The deep end

Where do fx and fy come from — the millimetres-to-pixels bridge

Your lens has a focal length in millimetres — 24 mm, 50 mm, whatever's on the barrel. But K's focal length is in pixels. The bridge is the sensor's pixel pitch:

fx = fmm / pixel_widthmm = fmm · (image_widthpx / sensor_widthmm)

So a 50 mm lens on a sensor with 5 µm pixels gives fx = 50 / 0.005 = 10 000 pixels. Two consequences fall out of this:

  • fx and fy differ only if pixels are non-square (pixel_width ≠ pixel_height). On almost all modern sensors pixels are square, so fx ≈ fy. The famous exception is anamorphic cinema, which squeezes the image horizontally on purpose. Some textbooks even write a single f and an aspect ratio α with fy = α·fx.
  • The same lens has different fx on different cameras. Crop the sensor or change the resolution and the pixel count per millimetre changes — which is exactly why the next page's resize rule scales the focal length.

source Hartley & Zisserman §6.1; OpenCV calibration docs.

The skew term, written out (and why it's basically always zero)

Skew models a sensor whose pixel axes aren't at 90°. If the angle between the axes is θ, the Hartley–Zisserman form is

s = fx · cot(θ)

For a perfectly rectangular pixel grid, θ = 90°, cot(90°) = 0, and the term vanishes. It would only be non-zero on, say, a digitised photograph where the scan axes were sheared, or some exotic sensor geometry. In practice, calibration toolboxes default it to 0 and many don't even estimate it. We keep it in the matrix because it completes the algebra — and because a shear of the image is precisely the operation that switches it on.

note The cot(θ) form is H&Z's; sign conventions vary between texts.

Why K is upper-triangular: RQ decomposition

Start from a full 3×4 projection matrix P = [M | p] recovered from calibration, where the left 3×3 block M is invertible. We want to split it back into "lens" and "pose." The claim is that M = K·R with K upper-triangular and R orthogonal — and that's exactly what an RQ decomposition produces (RQ, not the more familiar QR: we want the triangular factor on the left).

The decomposition is unique once you fix signs by demanding a positive diagonal on K (a positive focal length is physically required). The orthogonal R is the camera's rotation; the leftover translation comes from p. The upper-triangular structure isn't imposed by hand — it's the only shape consistent with "an affine read-out of the image plane after a rigid rotation."

source Hartley & Zisserman §6.2.4; Simek, "Dissecting the Camera Matrix."
