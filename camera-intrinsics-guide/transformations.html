Crop, rescale, flip, rotate — one rule: K′ = A·K.

Almost every image edit you do in pixel space is an affine transform, and an affine transform on the image just left-multiplies K. Cropping shifts the principal point; resizing scales everything; flips and rotations are the same rule with a different A. Worked formulas and a live before/after.

One rule rules them all

Here's the punchline before the details, because it's genuinely this simple. Take any image-space edit that's an affine transform — a crop, a resize, a flip, a rotation, a shear, or any combination — and write it as a 3×3 matrix A acting on pixel coordinates. Then the camera you now have has intrinsics:

K′ = A · K

That's it. The transform left-multiplies K. The reason is a one-line chain you can verify in your head: a 3D point lands at pixel p = K·X; the edit moves it to p′ = A·p = A·K·X = (A·K)·X. The new "stuff that turns world points into pixels" is the product A·K. Crop, scale, flip, rotate — they're not five rules, they're one rule with five different A's.

The mental model

K maps the normalized plane onto your sensor. An affine edit is just a second linear read-out stacked on top — re-gridding the sensor. Two linear read-outs in a row compose into one, and that one is A·K. The extrinsics [R | t] never enter; the world hasn't moved, only your sensor's coordinate system.

Order matters: it's A·K, not K·A. Putting A on the right would mean "transform the normalized plane before the lens sees it," which is a different, usually meaningless, operation. The edit happens after projection, so it goes on the left. Let's specialise it.

Cropping: only the principal point moves

Cropping away the top-left ox columns and oy rows is a pure translation of the pixel grid. Its A is the identity with an offset, and when you multiply it through:

10−ox 01−oy 001
A for a crop

fx′ = fx
fy′ = fy
cx′ = cx − ox
cy′ = cy − oy

The focal lengths don't budge — a crop doesn't change how many pixels a metre spans, it just throws pixels away. Only the principal point shifts, and it shifts by exactly the offset you removed. Note the minus sign: chop 120 pixels off the left and the optical centre, which used to be at cx = 240, is now at column 120 in the smaller image. Cropping moves the centre toward the corner you cut from. Centre-cropping is just this with ox, oy chosen to trim both sides equally.

The bug this prevents

If you crop an image and feed it to anything that uses K — a pose estimator, an AR overlay, a stereo rig — and you forget to subtract the offset from cx, cy, every reprojection is silently wrong by the crop amount. This is one of the most common calibration bugs in the wild.

Rescaling: every entry scales

Resize by (sx, sy) — say 0.5 to halve resolution — and A is a diagonal scaling. Multiply through and everything in the affected row scales, principal point included:

sx00 0sy0 001
A for a resize

fx′ = sx·fx
fy′ = sy·fy
cx′ = sx·cx
cy′ = sy·cy

This is the one people get wrong by halves. The instinct is "I resized, so scale the focal length" — and then they forget the principal point also scales. If your centre was at (240, 180) and you halve the image, it's now at (120, 90), not still (240, 180). A uniform resize (sx = sy) keeps the picture looking identical and just relabels the grid; a non-uniform one (sx ≠ sy) genuinely stretches it and pulls fx and fy apart — the same anamorphic effect that makes pixels non-square.

The half-pixel trap (why cx′ = sx·cx is slightly wrong)

The clean rule cx′ = sx·cx assumes integer pixel coordinates sit at pixel corners. But most libraries (OpenCV, and PyTorch/TensorFlow with the half-pixel convention) treat integer coordinates as pixel centres — pixel 0 covers the span [−0.5, +0.5]. Under that convention the geometrically exact map is:

cx′ = sx · (cx + 0.5) − 0.5

The +0.5 … −0.5 dance converts centre-referenced to corner-referenced and back. For large images the difference is sub-pixel and often ignored; for repeated down/up-sampling, or any precise geometric task, skipping it injects a creeping half-pixel shift that ruins calibration. Sources genuinely disagree on the default convention — which is exactly why you should pin yours down rather than trust a formula you read once.

source Richeimer, "Resize Confusion"; OpenCV resize semantics.

The classical move: a general affine

Crop and scale are the gentle special cases. The "classical operation" worth internalising is the general 2D affine — the umbrella that contains flip, rotation-in-the-image-plane, and shear. Its A is a full 2×3:

abtx cdty 001
A for any affine — flip, rotate, shear all live here

The same K′ = A·K applies. Three instances worth knowing by feel:

  • Horizontal flip of a W-wide image: A = [[−1, 0, W], [0, 1, 0]]. The −1 mirrors x; the +W shoves the mirrored image back into positive coordinates. (Use W−1 if your origin is the centre of pixel 0 rather than its corner — the perennial off-by-one.)
  • Shear: A = [[1, k, 0], [0, 1, 0]]. This one is special — it writes a value straight into K's skew slot. The skew term you'll "never see in a real camera" is exactly what a sheared image looks like.
  • Rotation in the image plane: A = [[cos, −sin, …], [sin, cos, …]]. Here's the catch — see the callout.
Rotation breaks the tidy shape

An in-plane rotation is a perfectly legal affine, so K′ = A·K still holds — but the product is no longer upper-triangular: the lower-left entry goes non-zero. It's still a valid linear camera, just not in canonical form. To recover clean fx, fy, cx, cy you'd RQ-decompose A·K and absorb the rotation into the extrinsics — which makes sense: rotating the photo is indistinguishable from having rolled the camera. So what's the boundary?

Every operation on this page shares one property: it sends straight lines to straight lines. That's the secret signature of "can be folded into K." Crop, scale, flip, rotate, shear — all of them preserve straightness, so all of them are just another matrix you can multiply in. The moment an operation bends a straight line, no A exists, and K′ = A·K has nothing to say. That's the next page.
