Camera intrinsics: from XYZ in the world to a pixel.

The intrinsic matrix K is the camera's own fingerprint — the part of projection that has nothing to do with where the camera is, only how it turns rays into pixels. We'll nail down exactly what K does, play with every knob, and then watch what happens to it when you crop, resize, warp, or distort the image.

Your instinct is right — with one asterisk

You asked: does the matrix actually move a point from XYZ in space into pixel space? Yes. That is exactly what it does. The asterisk is which XYZ, and the fact that one step of the journey is sneakily not a matrix multiply at all.

Projection happens in stages, and it helps to name them before we touch a single number:

Which raises the question you're already asking: can you run this backwards? Mostly no, and the diagram's lower track shows precisely why. The two matrix steps are each perfectly invertible — K⁻¹ exists (K is a full-rank upper-triangular matrix), and a rigid [R | t] undoes itself. The culprit is the ÷Z wedged between them: it throws depth away. Every point along a ray maps to the same pixel, so a pixel back-projects to a whole ray, not a point. To pin down where on that ray the surface actually is, you need depth from somewhere else — which is exactly the job LiDAR does.

The precise answer

K maps a point in the camera's own 3D coordinate frame to pixel coordinates. Not the world frame — the extrinsics [R | t] handle world → camera first. And K only finishes the job after the perspective divide by depth Z. That divide is the one genuinely non-linear step, and it's the reason a camera is a camera and not a photocopier. K itself is pure linear algebra; the "3D → 2D" magic lives in the ÷Z.

There are two equivalent ways to say where K acts, and both are worth holding in your head:

  • Frame A — K eats a 3D camera-frame point. Multiply K · (X, Y, Z) to get a homogeneous pixel (ũ, ṽ, w), then divide by w = Z to land on the real pixel (u, v).
  • Frame B — K eats a flattened 2D point. Do the divide first: every point on a ray collapses to (X/Z, Y/Z, 1) on the normalized image plane one unit in front of the lens. K then linearly maps that plane into pixels. This is the cleaner mental model: K is the change of coordinates from "metric units on the unit plane" to "pixels on the sensor."

They give the same answer because K·(X,Y,Z)/Z = K·(X/Z, Y/Z, 1) — K commutes with the scalar divide. Frame B is the one to remember, because it makes the next three pages obvious: everything K does is a 2D affine read-out of a flat plane, and everything you can do to that read-out is another 2D affine.

Why LiDAR pairs with a camera

A camera measures direction but discards range; LiDAR measures range directly. That's why they're so often bolted together — each supplies what the other can't. LiDAR returns 3D points in its own sensor frame, and a one-off LiDAR→camera calibration gives a rigid [R | t] (a rotation and translation, fully invertible) that drops those points into the camera frame. From there it works both directions: project LiDAR points through K into the image to colour or densify the cloud (3D → pixel, the easy way), or lift pixels to 3D by reading each pixel's depth off the aligned LiDAR (pixel → 3D, the inverse a lone camera can't do). The depth the ÷Z destroyed is exactly the number LiDAR hands back.
