Where it fails, changing backgrounds, and the drone problem

The same things that make correlation filters fast make them brittle. A taxonomy of how they lose the target, a straight answer on whether they survive a changing background, a worked verdict on tracking a maneuvering drone, and the trackers that fixed each weakness.

How a correlation filter loses the target

Everything good about a correlation filter — it learns online, it searches locally, it updates every frame — is also exactly how it fails. The failures are not random; they’re the direct shadow of the design choices on the previous page. Six recurring ones:

  • Fast motion → out of the window. The tracker only correlates over a local search region around the last position. If the target (or the camera) jumps farther than that region between frames, the peak simply isn’t in the window and the target is gone. A bigger window costs speed and invites distractors.
  • Scale, and worse, aspect. Base KCF estimates translation only — the box never resizes. CSRT and DSST add a single isotropic scale factor. None of the classic filters estimate aspect-ratio change or out-of-plane rotation: the filter is a rigid rectangle. An object that changes its silhouette breaks that assumption (this is the heart of the drone problem).
  • Occlusion, with no way back. When something passes in front of the target, the peak drops and the position is wrong — and because there is no detector and no re-detection, a plain DCF tracker has no mechanism to recover once it’s truly lost. It keeps tracking something, just not your target.
  • Model contamination / drift. The online update retrains on whatever is inside the box every frame. Track slightly off-centre, or through a partial occlusion, and the filter learns background and occluder pixels. Errors compound, with no ground truth to correct them. Drift is the slow, silent killer.
  • Distractors. A second object that resembles the target — another player in the same kit, a similar texture, a reflection — produces its own peak. If it out-responds the true target for even a frame, the tracker hops to it and never looks back.
  • Boundary effects. The circulant/FFT trick assumes the patch wraps around periodically, which isn’t true and creates spurious periodic responses near the edges. This is what caps the usable search region; SRDCF and CSRT’s reliability map were built specifically to fight it.
The one that gets you

Drift from contaminated updates. The other five announce themselves — the peak collapses, the box jumps. Drift doesn’t. The filter keeps returning a confident peak while it slowly relearns itself onto the background or an occluder. By the time it’s obviously wrong, the “target” model is already mostly scenery. Watch peak height; a steady decline is your early warning.

Does it work over a changing background?

This is the question people most often get backwards, so here is the blunt version first: yes, a changing background is basically fine — far more fine than your intuition says — and the things that actually hurt you are not “change” at all. To see why, you have to notice what a correlation filter is not doing.

It is not background subtraction. A background-subtraction or frame-differencing system models the scene and flags whatever changed; it assumes a static camera and a stable background, and it falls apart the instant either moves. A correlation filter does the opposite: it localises the target by the target’s own learned appearance. It never models the background as a thing to hold constant. So a panning camera, a target flying past wildly different scenery, a moving crowd behind it — none of that inherently breaks the filter, because none of it is an assumption the filter relies on.

Breaks background subtraction

Camera pans. Scene lighting shifts. The whole background scrolls. A static-background model lights up everywhere — useless.

Fine for a correlation filter

Same pans, same scrolling scenery. The filter is still just looking for its peak. As long as the target stays in the search window and looks roughly like itself, the background behind it is irrelevant.

So where does background actually bite? Three specific places — and notice none of them is “the background changed”:

  • Background baked inside the box. Your initial box is a rectangle, but real targets aren’t. A bicycle, an outstretched limb, a thin antenna mast — a lot of the box is background. The filter learns those pixels too. While that background is constant they even help as free context. But the moment the scene behind the target changes, the part of the model built from the old background is now wrong, the peak weakens, and you drift. This is the real mechanism behind “changing background hurt my tracker.”
  • Distractors, not change. A changing background is dangerous mainly when the new scenery happens to contain something that looks like the target. The failure is resemblance, not motion: pan from empty sky onto a tree-line full of dark blobs and one of them may out-peak your dark target.
  • Change driven by fast camera motion. If the background is changing because the camera is whipping around, the target’s apparent motion may exceed the search window — and now you’ve lost it for the fast-motion reason, not the background reason.
The key reframing

Changing background ≠ problem. Confusing background = problem. A correlation filter is essentially immune to scenery change in principle, but vulnerable to (1) look-alike distractors and (2) background pixels trapped inside a rectangular box. This is precisely what CSRT’s spatial reliability map fixes: by masking the filter down to the object’s actual shape, it stops learning the in-box background in the first place — so if you care about changing backgrounds, reach for CSRT over KCF.

The drone problem — a worked verdict

You asked specifically about a moving drone that changes its cross-section relative to the camera. It’s a sharp test case, because a maneuvering drone trips almost every failure mode on this page at once. Let’s walk it through honestly, best case to worst.

Background: the one part that’s often easy

Against clear sky, a drone is close to the ideal target — high contrast, few distractors, a clean uncluttered response map. This is where DCF looks great. Against a tree-line, buildings, or ground clutter, it gets hard fast: low contrast, lots of dark blobs that act as distractors, and genuine confusers (birds, kites, balloons). Per the previous section, it’s the clutter and look-alikes that hurt, not the fact that the background scrolls as the drone crosses the frame.

Scale: a solvable problem

As the drone flies toward or away from the camera its apparent size changes. Base KCF has no scale estimation at all — the box stays one size, so either it fills with background as the drone shrinks, or it clips the drone as it grows; either way the model is being contaminated. CSRT (or DSST) estimates scale and handles this reasonably. So for a drone, CSRT is the floor, not a luxury.

Cross-section change: the one that actually breaks it

Here is the crux of your question. As a drone banks, yaws, and pitches, its silhouette changes — a head-on quad reads as a roughly square blob with four rotor masses; rolled onto its side or edge-on it collapses toward a thin line. Two things change together: the aspect ratio of the true bounding box, and the appearance itself (out-of-plane rotation reveals a different view).

A classic correlation filter estimates translation plus, at most, one isotropic scale. It cannot stretch the box from wide to tall, and it has no notion of the object having rotated in depth. So a filter trained on the head-on view correlates poorly with the edge-on view: the peak drops, confidence falls, and you’re one bad update away from drift. CSRT’s spatial mask re-estimates the object region each frame and softens the blow a little, but it does not solve aspect-ratio change — that capability simply isn’t in the model.

Honest about the evidence

That a banking drone breaks the rigid filter is an inference from two well-established facts — DCF can’t model aspect/out-of-plane change, and UAV targets are documented as small, low-texture, and appearance-shifting — rather than one cited experiment. What is measured: on the Anti-UAV410 benchmark, KCF is a weak baseline (≈ 34 on the reported state-accuracy metric) versus deeper trackers like ECO and SiamRPN (≈ 46). For drones, correlation filters are the baselines, not the winners.

Fast, erratic motion: the final straw

Drones dart. A quick repositioning can carry the target out of the local search window in a single frame, and combined with camera motion to keep it in view, the apparent jumps get larger still. Mitigations exist — a wider search region (slower, more distractors), higher capture frame rate (smaller per-frame motion), or a motion model (a Kalman predictor that moves the search window to where the drone is heading) — but each is a patch on a tracker that wasn’t built for it.

The verdict

KCF/CSRT can hold a drone through short, smooth, roughly-constant-aspect segments — great for a quick lock, a cheap first pass, or sky backgrounds. For sustained tracking through real maneuvers, pure DCF is not enough. Build the system as: CSRT as the floor (you need its scale estimation and mask), plus a motion model to steer the search window, plus re-detection — a small drone detector that re-acquires after loss (the TLD pattern). If you can afford the compute, a learned tracker (SiamRPN++ actually estimates aspect ratio; a transformer tracker; or SAM 2 for mask-level tracking) handles the cross-section change that DCF structurally cannot.

The variants — each one fixes a failure above

The history of correlation-filter tracking is basically a list of patches for the failures on this page. Read the table as “what did this one fix?”

Method Year Features Scale? What it fixed
MOSSE 2010 grayscale no the original — FFT filter at 669 fps
CSK 2012 raw pixels no added the kernel + circulant formalism
KCF 2015 HOG no HOG + kernel → accuracy at high speed
DSST 2014 HOG yes a separate 1-D scale filter
SRDCF 2015 HOG yes spatial regularisation → killed boundary effects, bigger search
CSR-DCF CSRT 2017 HOG + CN yes spatial + channel reliability → non-rectangular targets, less in-box background

Notice the throughline: scale (DSST), boundary effects (SRDCF), and in-box background / object shape (CSR-DCF) are each a named failure from the top of this page, addressed in turn. What none of them add is aspect-ratio estimation, re-detection, or offline-learned features — and those three gaps are exactly what the next generation went after.

What beats classic DCF today

Correlation filters didn’t die — they evolved, and in one important sense they never left at all.

  • Deep-feature DCF. C-COT (ECCV 2016) and ECO (CVPR 2017), both from Danelljan’s group, keep the correlation-filter machinery but swap HOG for deep CNN features, with a big accuracy jump at a real speed cost. The bridge between the hand-crafted era and the deep era.
  • Siamese trackers — correlation, reborn. SiamFC (Bertinetto et al., ECCV 2016 workshop) reframes tracking as cross-correlation between a learned embedding of the template and the search region — literally the same operation as a correlation filter, but with features learned offline on large video datasets instead of HOG. SiamRPN / SiamRPN++ (2018–19) bolt on a region-proposal head that estimates a full box — including aspect ratio, the thing classic DCF structurally cannot do. (Tellingly, Henriques, a KCF author, is also a SiamFC author.)
  • Transformer trackers. TransT (CVPR 2021), STARK (ICCV 2021), MixFormer (CVPR 2022), OSTrack, SeqTrack — attention replaces the correlation step for template–search matching, and these sit at the top of LaSOT and GOT-10k today.
  • SAM 2 (2024). The modern “click then track”: click an object, get a mask, and a streaming memory propagates that mask across the whole video. It outputs segmentation rather than a box, so a changing silhouette — the drone’s exact problem — is handled natively.
  • Long-term tracking via re-detection. TLD (Kalal, Mikolajczyk & Matas, TPAMI 2012) is the canonical pattern: pair a frame-to-frame tracker with a detector that re-acquires the target after loss. For anything that suffers full occlusion or leaves frame — drones very much included — this tracker-plus-detector structure is the robust answer, and it’s the same instinct behind detection-based multi-object trackers like ByteTrack.
The throughline

Cross-correlation never went away. MOSSE correlated a hand-built filter; KCF correlated HOG in a kernel; SiamFC correlates a learned embedding; transformer trackers generalise the match to attention. The 2010 insight — find the target by the sharp peak of a correlation response — is still beating inside the state of the art. What changed is where the features come from.

So: which should you reach for?

If you need speed, many targets, or a constant-size object: KCF. If you need robustness, scale, odd shapes, or you care about changing/cluttered backgrounds: CSRT — it’s the sensible default for most click-to-track tasks. If the target changes shape, vanishes and returns, or moves fast (drones, agile wildlife, sports through occlusion): a classic correlation filter alone won’t hold it — pair CSRT with a motion model and re-detection, or move to a learned tracker (SiamRPN++, a transformer tracker) or SAM 2. Match the tool to which failure on this page you’re actually going to hit.
